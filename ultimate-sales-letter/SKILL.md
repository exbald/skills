# The Ultimate Sales Letter: a step-by-step copy system

A paraphrased working version of the step system in Dan S. Kennedy's *The Ultimate Sales Letter*. The core idea: **selling copy is assembled, not invented.** The letter is a salesperson in print, so it has to do everything a good salesperson does in the room: get attention, find the prospect's problem, answer every question and objection, and ask for the order. You research the buyer and the offer first, then build, rewrite, test and ship in a fixed order.

Three beliefs run through the book and this skill:

- **Write to one person.** One specific reader with one specific problem, in a voice like one person writing to another.
- **Long copy wins when it stays interesting.** People read long copy about things they care about. Cut what is boring, not what is long. The piece is as long as the sale needs.
- **Every piece has one job.** One offer, one action. Anything that does not move the reader toward it comes out.

## Pick the mode

| The piece | Mode |
|---|---|
| A sales letter, long landing page, pricing page, launch or a full email sequence | **Full:** every phase below, all four deliverables |
| One email, one page section, an ad, a subject line, one post | **Light:** steps 1 to 3 as a short brief, step 6 (headline), steps 9, 12 and 13, then the final edit checklist. Skip the rest and say you skipped it. |

## Inputs to gather first

Do not write copy until these are filled in. Look in the user's materials first (docs, the product, existing pages, reviews, support threads), ask for the rest, and mark every gap `NEEDS INPUT`.

**The ten smart questions** (Kennedy's market diagnosis, paraphrased)

1. What keeps them awake at night? What problem is on their mind when nothing else is?
2. What are they afraid of?
3. What are they angry about, and who are they angry at?
4. What are their top three daily frustrations?
5. What trends are happening, or about to happen, in their work or life?
6. What do they secretly and most strongly want?
7. Do they have a built-in bias in how they decide (analytical, cautious, status-driven, price-first)?
8. Do they have their own vocabulary or jargon? Collect real phrases they use.
9. Who else is selling them something similar, and how?
10. Who else has tried to sell them this before, and why did it fail?

Also note who the reader is (role, situation), how they arrive at the copy (cold, referred, existing customer, from an ad promising X) and what they already believe.

**The product, from features to benefits**

- List every feature. For each, write the benefit ("so you can...") and rank the benefits by importance to the reader, not to the seller.
- Find the **hidden benefit**: the payoff the seller rarely mentions but the buyer cares about deeply (time back, status, looking good to the boss, peace of mind).

**Proof**

- Real testimonials (with permission), case studies, usage numbers, results, credentials, media, demos. Record the source of each item. No source means it cannot be used.

**The offer**

- Exactly what the buyer gets, the price and payment options, any guarantee or trial, bonuses, a deadline or limit (only if real), and the single action you want.
- Known flaws and limitations, and every reason someone might not respond.

## The process

The book numbers 28 steps (a later edition adds a 29th, on hiring a copywriter, which does not apply to an agent). Many steps were written for paper mail; each is translated below to its digital equivalent. Steps run in phase order, not number order.

### Phase 1: Research (steps 1 to 3)

1. **Get into the customer.** Answer the ten questions from real sources: user notes, reviews, support tickets, forums, sales-call notes, competitor reviews. Quote the buyer's own words where you can; their phrases become your headlines.
2. **Get into the offer.** Build the ranked feature-to-benefit table and name the hidden benefit. Understand the product well enough to explain why it is worth the price, and what the buyer loses by not having it.
3. **Write the damaging admission.** List every objection, doubt, fear and excuse. Then pick one honest drawback and say it out loud: "This is not for you if... but if you want X, it is." Admitting a real weakness makes the rest believable.

### Phase 2: Getting through, and the offer's shape (steps 4, 5, 7, 8)

4. **Get it delivered.** Make sure the copy reaches the right person: the right list, segment, channel and funnel step, past spam filters and gatekeepers. Kennedy's test is the **A-pile and the B-pile**: people sort their mail into what looks personal and important (read now) and what looks like advertising (bin later). Aim for the A-pile. In email that means a sender name that is a person, a plain look, and a subject line a colleague might write.
5. **Get it looked at.** Design the first touch that earns the open or the click. On paper this was the envelope teaser; now it is the subject line, preview text, ad, link preview or social card. It must promise exactly what the copy inside delivers, or the reader feels tricked and leaves.
7. **Beat the price objection.** Plan how price will be presented, before writing:
   - Build value before showing price. Make the parts feel worth more than the whole (the value stack).
   - Compare against the cost of the problem, not against zero.
   - Shrink the number: per day, per user, per result, or against something they already buy without thinking.
   - Explain what it took to build, where that is true and interesting.
   - Offer payment options where they exist.
   - Pick a structure that puts the problem before the price: problem, agitation, solution; a prediction of where things are heading; or a contrast between the winners who act and the losers who don't.
8. **Motivate action.** Choose the levers you can use honestly:
   - a real limit or qualification ("only for teams that...");
   - **selling money at a discount**: the return is plainly bigger than the price;
   - an appeal to ego or status;
   - a strong guarantee that takes the risk off the buyer;
   - a story the reader can see themselves in.

### Phase 3: Headline (step 6)

6. **Get it read.** The headline is an ad for the copy. Write ten or more using the formulas in the checklist, then shortlist three to five. Each must promise something the reader finds important, valuable and believable straight away, in the reader's own words. Flag the reader (name who it is for) in or above the headline. Treat the first paragraph as part of the headline: it either pulls the reader in or loses them. A short summary box at the top of a long piece (Kennedy's long letters often used one) can carry the offer for skimmers.

### Phase 4: First draft (steps 9, 12, 13, 14)

9. **Write the first draft.** Write fast and long, in blocks: hook, problem, agitation, solution, benefits, proof, damaging admission, offer, price, guarantee, urgency, call to action. Don't edit while drafting; there will be several rewrites.
12. **Answer questions and objections.** List every question a prospect would ask a salesperson across the desk, and answer each one in the copy, because nobody is there to answer it. For each objection from step 3, give a direct answer, a proof point or story, and a reminder of the guarantee or trial.
13. **Spark immediate action.** A reader who puts it down to "think about it" rarely comes back. Remove reasons to wait: a real deadline, a bonus for acting now, a fast-action price, and a response step that is as easy as possible. State exactly what to click or do next.
14. **Write the P.S.** Many readers look at the end first. Use the P.S. as a second headline (see the checklist).

### Phase 5: Rewrite and edit (steps 10, 11, 15 to 18, 21, 22)

10. **Rewrite for strategy.** Check the order of the argument. Build two reading paths: a skimmer who reads only the headline, subheads, bold lines, captions and P.S. should still get the promise, the offer and the action. Restate the core promise in several forms (plain statement, example, story, testimonial, numbered summary). End sections on a line that pulls into the next one.
11. **Rewrite for style.** Write how people talk: short words, sentences and paragraphs; concrete and sensory language; some personality. Make the first paragraph earn the second.
15. **Check the checklists.** Re-run the ten questions and every step against the draft. Fix what was skipped.
16. **Use graphic enhancement.** Subheads, bullets, bold for key lines, a boxed guarantee or offer, captions under images (captions get read), white space. Emphasise selectively: if everything stands out, nothing does.
17. **Rewrite for passion, then edit for clarity.** The writer's belief in the offer has to come through; add it where the copy reads cold. Then cut every word that doesn't advance the sale. You are cutting what readers would skip, not just making it shorter.
18. **Compare to strong examples.** Put the draft beside proven copy in the same format, from the user's swipe file or examples they supply, and note where yours is weaker. Kennedy keeps a swipe file of winners; ask the user for theirs.
21. **Change graphic enhancements.** Once the layout is real, adjust the emphasis and visuals so they support the argument instead of decorating it.
22. **Edit again.** Line by line, for clarity, accuracy and readability.

### Phase 6: Test and ship (steps 19, 20, 23 to 28)

19. **Pretest.** Read it aloud and fix anything you stumble on. Check the reading level; aim for plain language a young reader could follow. If possible, the user shows it to a few typical customers.
20. **Bring it to life.** Make it tangible, then see it in final form: a screenshot, demo, sample result or concrete before-and-after that the reader can picture (the digital version of the "grabbers" and inserts paper letters used), rendered as the real page, email or post, not a text file.
23. **Send a mockup.** Send the email to a test inbox, or open the page on a phone and a desktop. Check subject truncation, preview text, and how it looks among the other messages in an inbox.
24. **Let it cool.** Recommend leaving it for a day or more before the final edits. If time allows, come back to it in a fresh pass yourself.
25. **Get second opinions.** Suggest specific reviewers (a teammate, a customer, another writer) and the questions to ask them ("What is this selling? Would you act? What made you hesitate?").
26. **Final review.** One last distraction-free read against the final edit checklist.
27. **Go to press.** Hand off the final copy. The user publishes or sends; never publish anything yourself without explicit approval.
28. **Test.** Propose a test plan: one variable at a time (headline, offer, price framing, bonus, guarantee, image), what to measure, and when to read the result. Keep the winner as the new control and test against it. Plan follow-ups: one message rarely gets every sale, so outline a short sequence for those who didn't respond.

## Checklists

**Headline formulas (fill in the blanks)**

- They didn't think I could ___, but I did.
- Who else wants ___?
- How ___ made me ___.
- Are you ___?
- How I ___.
- How to ___.
- Secrets of ___.
- Thousands now ___ even though they ___.
- Warning: ___.
- Give me ___ and I'll ___.
- [Number] ways to ___.
- To flag the reader, put "For [specific audience] who [specific problem]:" above any of these.

**Making the offer stronger**

- **Guarantee:** money back, refund and keep the bonus, free trial, or make the guarantee the centrepiece. Repeat it in several places. Use only terms the user confirms.
- **Deadline or limit:** only if real. State the date or number and why it exists.
- **Bonuses:** premiums that add value and are worth more than they cost to deliver; consider a fast-action bonus.
- **Damaging admission:** name one real drawback and who it rules out.
- **Price justification:** value stack, cost of the problem, return on investment, per-day math, cost to develop, payment options, comparison with alternatives.
- **Ease of response:** one clear action, minimal steps, no surprise fields or costs.

**What the P.S. can do**

- Restate the main promise or biggest benefit as a second headline.
- Restate the offer and guarantee in one or two lines.
- Remind them of the deadline or bonus.
- Add one strong proof point or story teaser.
- Give the exact next step.

**Final edit checklist**

- [ ] The headline promises a clear, believable benefit to a specific reader.
- [ ] The first paragraph pulls into the body.
- [ ] The skimmer's path works on its own.
- [ ] Benefits lead; features support.
- [ ] The hidden benefit appears.
- [ ] The damaging admission is included.
- [ ] Every objection from step 3 is answered, with proof.
- [ ] Price comes after value is built, with a justification.
- [ ] The guarantee is stated clearly and repeated.
- [ ] Any urgency is real and explained.
- [ ] One clear call to action, repeated and easy to take.
- [ ] The P.S. is there and doing a job.
- [ ] Plain language and short sentences; it reads well aloud.
- [ ] Every claim, number and testimonial is traced to a source, or flagged.
- [ ] The first touch (subject line, preview, card) matches what the copy delivers.
- [ ] No em dashes in public copy.

## Applying it to SaaS copy

- **Landing pages:** the Phase 3 headline above the fold, with a subhead that flags the reader. Problem and agitation before features. Feature sections written as benefits. Proof next to every claim. The damaging admission as a "who this is for, and who it isn't for" block. An FAQ built from the step 12 question list. Repeat the call to action after each major section. A free tool or instant demo counts as the offer: the action is "try it", and the copy still has to sell the try.
- **Pricing pages:** step 7 in public. Anchor each plan to the outcome and the cost of the problem, show what's included as a value stack, recommend one plan for a named buyer type, state the trial or refund terms plainly, and answer billing objections right beside the price.
- **Launch posts:** lead with the reader's problem or a news hook, not the company. Tell the story behind the product, show one concrete before and after, include a real launch offer or deadline if one exists, and end with a single action and a P.S.-style closing line.
- **Email sequences:** each email is a short sales letter with its subject line as its headline, and it should land in the A-pile. Plan a timed series (for example: welcome, problem story, proof, objection handling, offer, deadline reminder). Each email adds a new reason to act instead of repeating the last one. Write separate follow-ups for trial users who didn't convert.

## Deliverables (full mode)

Return these in order:

1. **Research brief:** answers to the ten questions, the ranked benefit table with the hidden benefit, the objection and question list, the damaging admission, an offer summary, and a list of `NEEDS INPUT` gaps.
2. **Headline options:** three to five shortlisted headlines, each with the formula used and a one-line rationale. Mark the one you recommend.
3. **Full draft:** the complete copy in its final layout with subheads, bullets, call to action and P.S. Use `[PROOF NEEDED: ...]` wherever a testimonial, number or claim has to come from the user.
4. **Edit checklist results:** each item in the final edit checklist marked pass, fail or flagged, with a note on any failure, plus the step 28 test plan.

In light mode, return a short brief, the headline options and the draft, then the checklist results.

## Rules

- Never invent testimonials, customer names, quotes, statistics, results, ratings, awards or client lists. Use placeholders and ask.
- Never invent offer terms: guarantees, deadlines, scarcity, discounts and bonuses must come from the user. Fake urgency is not allowed.
- Flag every claim that needs proof, and list all flags at the end of the draft so the user can supply or remove them.
- Keep claims specific and supportable. Avoid health, financial or legal promises unless the user provides substantiation.
- No em dashes in public-facing copy. Use commas, periods, colons or parentheses.
- Don't name competitors in the copy unless the user explicitly asks and supplies accurate facts.
- Project rules win: if the repo or brand has a copy guide, banned words, a fixed descriptor or a "no tagline" rule, follow it over anything here.
- Don't publish, send or post anything. Deliver drafts for the user to approve.
